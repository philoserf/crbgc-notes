---
title: "Golf Measures"
date: 2026-06-10
---

A scorecard records the result and hides the cause. To know why a round came out the way it did—and what to practice next—you need a handful of measures that decompose the score into the few behaviors that actually drive it. The temptation is to track everything; the discipline is to track little. The six below are chosen because each isolates a distinct skill, each is easy to mark on the card as you play, and together they cover the tee, the approach, the short game, and the putter without overlap. Record them every round, ignore single-round noise, and read the running averages.

## GIR — greens in regulation

A green is hit "in regulation" when the ball is on the putting surface with at least two putts left to make par: the first shot on a par 3, the second on a par 4, the third on a par 5. GIR is the single best summary of ball-striking, because hitting greens is what separates scoring tiers more reliably than any other count. It rolls tee shot and approach into one honest number. Mark a simple ✓ per hole and total them. As a rough ladder: a bogey golfer hits a few greens a round, a mid-handicap player hits five to eight, and single digits start around nine or ten. Watch the trend, not the round.

## Driver distance

Carry plus roll off the tee, averaged over the drives where you made normal contact. The point is not ego; it is to know your real number so you can pick targets and clubs honestly. Most amateurs overestimate by a club or more because they remember their best strike, not their median. Measure with a rangefinder or GPS from tee to where the ball actually came to rest on a handful of flat, into-neutral-wind holes, and trust the average. Distance matters mostly because it shortens approach shots, which is what feeds GIR—so read this number together with GIR, never alone.

## Trouble off the tee

A count of tee shots that cost a stroke or forced a recovery: out of bounds, a penalty hazard, a fairway bunker you can only wedge out of, trees with no shot. This is the other half of driving, and for most players it matters more than distance. A long drive in the fairway and a long drive in the trees are not the same skill. Tally one mark per offending hole. Falling trouble counts almost always lower scores faster than rising distance does, because eliminating the blow-up hole protects the whole round. When the count is high, the fix is usually club selection and a wider target, not a swing change.

## Three putts

Greens where you took three putts or more. Three-putts are the cheapest strokes to recover because they come from two correctable causes: poor speed control on the long first putt, or weak distance judgment that leaves an awkward second. They also compound the cost of a good approach—hitting a green in regulation means little if it becomes a three-putt bogey. Most three-putts trace to the lag putt, not the short one, so the practice that moves this number is distance control from long range, not more reps from three feet.

## Pitch and chip within 50 yards

Track up-and-down conversion from inside fifty yards: when you miss the green from short range, how often do you get down in two—one pitch or chip, one putt? This is the score-saver, the skill that turns missed greens into pars and keeps a bad ball-striking day from becoming a bad scoring day. Mark each greenside attempt and whether you saved par. The amateur scoring leak lives here as much as anywhere: a player who hits few greens but converts a healthy share of these up-and-downs scores like someone two handicap tiers better.

## Missed putts within five feet

The short putts you expected to make and missed. Inside five feet is where confidence and routine live, and a missed three-footer costs exactly as much as a missed thirty-footer while feeling far worse. Counting them separately keeps the failure visible instead of buried in a total putt count, and it isolates a skill—starting the ball on line under a little pressure—that responds quickly to focused practice. A rising number here often signals a routine or alignment problem rather than a stroke problem.

---

Read the six together. GIR and driver distance describe how you get to the green; trouble off the tee describes how you fail to; three-putts, the short-game saves, and the missed short putts describe what happens once you are near it. A round's score is mostly the sum of these. Track them for a month, average them, and the one number that is worst relative to your handicap tells you what to practice—no guessing required.
