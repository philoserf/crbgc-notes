A fixed weekly team league is strongest if your main goal is building a durable social institution. Everyone knows when league night is, people see the same group regularly, teams create identity, and attendance becomes habitual. It is easy to explain and easy to administer. Its weakness is rigidity: missed weeks, substitutes, uneven attendance, and rescheduling can become the organizer's burden. It also tends to feel more like "league golf" than pure match play unless the scoring is designed carefully.

A round robin followed by a knockout match-play championship is stronger if your main goal is competitive integrity. Everyone gets several guaranteed matches, the regular season matters, and the championship has a clean dramatic finish. It is also easier to seed fairly than a ladder because players have faced a defined set of opponents rather than selectively challenging one another.

For a new league, that format may actually be the best starting point.

I would structure it something like this: divide the field into groups of perhaps four to six players. Each player has a defined window in which to complete a match against every other player in the group. Matches can be scheduled flexibly rather than on a fixed night. Standings are based on wins, halves, and losses. The top players from each group advance to a seeded knockout championship.

For example, with 24 players:

Four groups of six.

Each player gets five regular-season matches.

Win = 1 point.  
Halve = ½ point.  
Loss = 0.

Top two from each group advance.

Eight-player knockout bracket.

Quarterfinals, semifinals, final.

That gives every player meaningful participation without requiring a 23-match full round robin.

A regular league evening—say every second Tuesday—can run alongside this without requiring that all matches happen then, which keeps the social advantages of a fixed league while retaining scheduling flexibility.

I would rank the three models this way for a new league.

If community and routine come first: fixed weekly team league.

If competition and fairness come first: round robin or pool play into a knockout championship.

If flexibility and continuous informal play come first: ladder.

For a club starting from nothing, pool play plus a knockout championship beats a ladder. It has cleaner competitive logic, produces a real season, guarantees matches, and culminates in something everyone immediately understands.

The ladder could still exist as a secondary, year-round activity rather than the league's primary structure.
