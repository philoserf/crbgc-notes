A match-play league takes the same hole-by-hole scoring system and stretches it across a season rather than using a single-elimination bracket.

The most common structure is a round-robin or scheduled series of head-to-head matches. Each golfer plays a different opponent each week, and the result of each match contributes to season standings.

A league might award:

- 1 point for a win
- ½ point for a halved match
- 0 points for a loss

After ten weeks, a player might therefore be 6–2–2: six wins, two losses, and two halves, for 7 league points.

Some leagues instead award points by hole. In that format, every hole matters to the season standings. A nine-hole match might offer 9 points, one per hole. If Player A wins five holes, Player B wins three, and one is halved, the result would be 5½–3½.

Another common system combines both ideas. For example:

- 1 point for each hole
- 2 additional points for winning the overall match

That rewards both consistency across the round and actually defeating the opponent.

Handicaps are especially important in recreational match-play leagues. Players usually receive strokes according to the difference between their course handicaps. If one player has a course handicap of 12 and the other 6, the 12-handicap player receives six strokes, generally on the six lowest handicap-indexed holes.

So on a hole where the higher-handicap player receives a stroke:

Player A makes 5 gross.  
Player B makes 4 gross.

If Player A receives a stroke there, Player A's net score is 4. The hole is halved.

This makes match-play leagues unusually good at mixing players of different abilities.

Leagues can also be individual or team-based. A two-person team league might have something like:

Player A vs Player A = 1 point  
Player B vs Player B = 1 point  
Team aggregate or four-ball result = 1 point

So three league points are available in each weekly fixture.

At the end of the regular season, the league might simply crown the player or team with the most points, or it might use the standings to seed a playoff bracket.

The important distinction from a match-play tournament is therefore this:

A tournament asks, "Did you win this match and advance?"

A league asks, "How did you perform across many matches over the season?"

That makes league design much more flexible. The organizer has to decide whether the standings should reward winning matches, winning individual holes, total net performance, or some combination of them.
